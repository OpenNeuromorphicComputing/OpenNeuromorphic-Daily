### [Correcting topographic artifacts in three-dimensional ToF-SIMS: A practical guide from data-only reconstruction to correlative AFM](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28593v1)
**2026-10-20** | *Jacob Shusterman, Anton V. Ievlev*

> Three-dimensional time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (3D ToF-SIMS) builds chemical volumes by stacking images acquired between sputter cycles. Standard reconstruction assumes a flat starting surface and a single sputter rate, so samples with topography, or with layers that sputter at different rates, yield distorted interface positions and depth scales. This article is a practical guide to correcting these artifacts. We group the available corrections by the information they need: the SIMS data alone, atomic force microscopy (AFM) topography before and after the profile, or AFM topography also at intermediate stages. All three are applied to a model Pt/TaOx/Ta/Pt/Ti crossbar memristor device measured with in situ SIMS/AFM at ten stages, together with the data preparation they share. AFM before and after the profile fixes the start and end surfaces but assumes uniform sputtering in between, which fails next to the device, where the surroundings change material during the profile. Multi-stage AFM correction follows these changes and reproduces the structure of the device, at the cost of instrument time and repeated imaging of a partially sputtered surface. A decision flowchart and a checklist help readers choose, validate and report a correction.

### [Migrate Computation, Not Data: Fault Recovery in Neuromorphic Many-Core Systems](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26985v1)
**2026-10-19** | *Muyang Cao, Di Yu, Peiling Zhu et al.*

> As neuromorphic systems scale to thousands of near-memory cores, aging-induced permanent faults in neuron-update datapaths become an important obstacle to long-term deployment. Existing online recovery methods typically retire a faulty core and migrate its neurons, along with their weights and membrane states, to healthy cores. This whole-core treatment incurs high recovery traffic and makes recovery depend on scarce destinations; at high utilization, there may be enough surviving compute capacity but insufficient free storage to copy the affected neuron context. This paper presents SpikeRelay, an online recovery framework for diagnosed compute-unit faults. SpikeRelay exploits the fact that, in many neuromorphic cores, the compute datapath, local SRAM, and router are distinct modules: a compute fault need not destroy stored weights and states or disconnect the core from the NoC. Instead of retiring a faulty core and migrating its neuron data, SpikeRelay converts the faulty core into a storage-and-forwarding node while offloading neuron-update computation to a healthy rescue core. This decoupling of storage and computation eliminates recovery-time data migration and greatly reduces spare-SRAM requirements. Consequently, SpikeRelay sustains 100\% recovery at 92.2\% SRAM utilization and a 50\% fault rate, whereas the best baseline achieves only 22.1\%.
